**A DSCR loan prepayment penalty is a fee you pay if you sell or refinance the property before a set period ends. Common structures step down each year, such as 5-4-3-2-1 over five years or 3-2-1 over three. Choosing a shorter penalty usually costs a slightly higher rate, so match the term to your exit plan.**

Most investors focus on rate, down payment, and the DSCR ratio. The prepayment penalty can matter just as much, especially if you plan to refinance after a rehab or sell within a few years. Here's how these penalties typically work and how to choose one.

## Key Takeaways

- **Many DSCR loans include a prepayment penalty,** often called a "prepay." It's common because DSCR loans are business-purpose loans on investment property.
- **Step-down structures are typical.** A 5-4-3-2-1 penalty charges 5% of the amount prepaid in year one, 4% in year two, and so on.
- **Shorter or no prepay usually means a higher rate,** and a longer prepay can lower your rate.
- **State rules can limit prepayment penalties,** depending on the state, loan size, and whether you borrow in your own name or an LLC.
- **Terms vary by lender.** The structures here are common industry examples, not Coastal Funding program terms.

## What Is a DSCR Prepayment Penalty?

A prepayment penalty is a charge for paying off all or a large part of a loan early. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-prepayment-penalty-en-1957/) suggests reading the fine print and asking for a quote on a similar loan without a prepayment penalty, so you can compare total costs.

Owner-occupied home loans face strict federal limits on prepayment penalties. Business-purpose credit, including DSCR loans on rental property, is generally exempt from the Truth in Lending Act under [Regulation Z section 1026.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1026/subpart-A/section-1026.3). That's why DSCR penalties are mainly shaped by the lender's program, your note, and state law.

## How Do Step-Down Prepayment Penalties Work?

The penalty percentage usually applies to the amount you prepay. Here's what common structures look like on a $300,000 balance:

| Year paid off | 5-4-3-2-1 | 3-2-1 | No prepay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 5% = $15,000 | 3% = $9,000 | $0 |
| Year 2 | 4% = $12,000 | 2% = $6,000 | $0 |
| Year 3 | 3% = $9,000 | 1% = $3,000 | $0 |
| Year 4 | 2% = $6,000 | $0 | $0 |
| Year 5 | 1% = $3,000 | $0 | $0 |
| Year 6+ | $0 | $0 | $0 |

Other structures exist, including flat percentages, such as 3% for three years, and interest-based penalties. Some notes let you prepay a portion of the balance each year without a penalty. Always read the exact terms.

## What Triggers a DSCR Prepayment Penalty?

Typically, a penalty applies when you:

- **Sell the property** and pay off the loan.
- **Refinance,** including a cash-out refinance.
- **Pay off the loan in full** with cash or from another property.

Regular monthly payments don't trigger a penalty. Small extra principal payments usually don't either, but check your note. Some lenders also waive the penalty in specific cases, such as a sale after a casualty loss. Ask before you close.

## Do States Limit Prepayment Penalties on Investment Loans?

Yes, some do. Rules can depend on the property type, the loan amount, the interest rate, and whether the borrower is an individual or an entity. Because of this, DSCR lenders keep state-by-state matrices. In some states, a lender may offer no prepay at all, or only when you close in an LLC. Your loan officer should confirm what's allowed in your property's state before you lock.

## How Do I Choose the Right Prepayment Term?

Match the penalty to your most likely exit:

- **Long-term hold (5+ years):** A 5-year prepay often makes sense if it lowers your rate. You may never pay the penalty.
- **BRRRR or value-add:** If you plan to refinance after a rehab, a short prepay or no prepay can save thousands. See our guide to [DSCR cash-out refinance and BRRRR](/posts/dscr-cash-out-refinance-brrrr/).
- **Possible 1031 exchange:** If you might sell and exchange within a few years, factor the penalty into your net proceeds. Our [year-end 1031 exchange guide](/posts/dscr-loan-1031-exchange-year-end/) covers the timing rules.
- **Uncertain plans:** A 3-2-1 can be a middle ground between rate and flexibility.

## How Much Rate Do You Trade for a Shorter Prepay?

Pricing differs by lender and market, so compare actual quotes. Ask for the same loan priced with a 5-year, 3-year, 1-year, and no prepay. Then compare the extra monthly interest with the penalty you'd likely pay at your planned exit.

## What Questions Should I Ask a DSCR Lender?

- What prepayment structures are available, and how does each change my rate?
- Is the penalty based on the original amount or the amount prepaid?
- Can I prepay part of the balance each year without a penalty?
- Does the penalty apply if I sell, or only if I refinance?
- What does my property's state allow for individuals versus LLCs?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do all DSCR loans have prepayment penalties?

No. Many DSCR loans include one, but lenders often offer shorter terms or no prepayment penalty, usually at a higher interest rate. State rules can also limit them.

### What does a 5-4-3-2-1 prepayment penalty mean?

It means you'd pay 5% of the amount prepaid in year one, 4% in year two, 3% in year three, 2% in year four, and 1% in year five. After five years, there's no penalty.

### Can I avoid a DSCR prepayment penalty?

You can choose a no-prepay or shorter-prepay option, often in exchange for a higher rate. You can also hold the property until the penalty period ends, or use any partial prepayment allowance in your note.

### Does refinancing a DSCR loan trigger the prepayment penalty?

Usually yes. Refinancing pays off the original loan, so it typically triggers the penalty during the prepay period. Check your note for the exact terms.

### Is a prepayment penalty on a rental property tax-deductible?

Prepayment penalties on rental property loans may be deductible, but the treatment depends on your situation. Ask a CPA or tax professional before you rely on a deduction.
